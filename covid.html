Lonely Together — Tracking Employee Experience During COVID-19

A three-phase longitudinal study examining how social distancing and work uncertainty were associated with loneliness and work engagement — using baseline measurement, 14 daily pulse surveys, and a post-study outcome survey.

My role

I was the lead author and owned the core research process: idea generation, research design, questionnaire development, data cleaning and analysis, and the major theory, methods, and discussion writing. The Bureau of Sociological Research supported recruitment, IRB logistics, survey deployment, and participant compensation.

The research question

Social distancing was necessary for public health, but it also changed how employees connected with other people. We asked whether those day-to-day distancing behaviors were associated with loneliness, whether repeated exposure accumulated into more persistent loneliness, and whether work uncertainty made those associations stronger.

Study design

The project used experience sampling because the question depended on both short-term fluctuation and longer-term accumulation. A one-time survey would not separate “today I feel lonelier than usual” from “I am generally experiencing more loneliness across the study period.”

Why experience sampling?

  • Capture experiences close to when they occur
  • Reduce retrospective recall bias
  • Model within-person and between-person processes simultaneously
  • Observe whether short-term experiences accumulate over time

Fieldwork

  • Probability-based Nebraska panel as the sampling frame
  • Baseline survey before the daily study
  • 14 daily surveys sent over two weeks
  • Post-study work engagement survey
  • Qualtrics deployment with structured reminders and participant incentives

The multilevel structure was the point of the design

Analysis workflow

The study generated 17 datasets that had to be merged, recoded, checked, and structured for repeated-measures analysis. I used R for data manipulation and then evaluated nonresponse bias, variance decomposition, measurement validity, and the multilevel mediation/moderation models.

What we learned

Daily experience

On days when employees reported more social distancing, they also reported more loneliness. This was a within-person association: the same individual could be more or less lonely depending on that day’s distancing experience.

Cumulative experience

Employees with higher average social distancing across the study also reported more average loneliness, and higher average loneliness was associated with lower work engagement after the daily-survey period.

Work uncertainty changed the longer-run story

Work uncertainty did not significantly strengthen the day-level distancing–loneliness relationship, but it did strengthen the between-person association between average distancing and average loneliness. The indirect association with work engagement was present under higher work uncertainty and not under lower work uncertainty.

Research rigor

  • Nonresponse bias checks
  • ICC / variance decomposition
  • Multilevel confirmatory factor analysis
  • Lagged daily loneliness control
  • Within- and between-person covariates
  • Mediation and moderated-mediation tests

Why this is relevant to employee listening

The study shows how to design listening around time rather than treating employee experience as static. Repeated measurement makes it possible to distinguish daily fluctuation from persistent experience and to identify when contextual uncertainty changes the meaning of the same workplace exposure.

Why this project matters: it demonstrates end-to-end ownership of a longitudinal employee-listening study — research question, questionnaire design, fielding strategy, repeated-measures data engineering, multilevel modeling, and careful interpretation of both significant and non-significant findings.
