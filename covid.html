PRIMARY RESEARCH · LONGITUDINAL EMPLOYEE EXPERIENCE

How social disconnection relates to employee engagement.

I led a longitudinal employee experience study examining how social distancing, loneliness, and work uncertainty related to subsequent work engagement. The findings raise questions relevant to employee listening and distributed work.

01 / THE RESEARCH PROBLEM

What can one-time engagement surveys miss?

A single survey can obscure the difference between a difficult day and persistent loneliness. I designed repeated measurement to examine how daily experiences and more sustained patterns relate to subsequent engagement, and how work uncertainty enters the picture.

02 / Longitudinal investigation

Three phases make it possible to connect daily experience with later reported engagement, while distinguishing within-person from between-person variation.

03 / WHAT WE FOUND

Persistent disconnection was linked to engagement.

01 / DAILY EXPERIENCE

Distancing and loneliness fluctuated together

On days when an employee reported more distancing than usual, they also reported more loneliness.

Stakeholder implication

Behavioral separation may accompany psychological disconnection. Consider checking in on employees’ sense of connection, not only their physical work arrangements.

02 / PERSISTENT EXPERIENCE

Loneliness mattered beyond a single day

Higher average distancing was associated with higher average loneliness; higher average loneliness was associated with lower subsequent work engagement.

Stakeholder implication

Persistent distancing and loneliness deserve attention rather than an assumption that disconnection will resolve on its own. Monitor sustained experiences and explore appropriate support.

03 / CONTEXT

Uncertainty changed the pattern

Work uncertainty strengthened the between-person distancing–loneliness association, but not the daily association.

Stakeholder implication

Consider work uncertainty when assessing disconnection, and explore ways to reduce avoidable uncertainty for employees.

04 / BUSINESS IMPLICATIONS

The potential hidden costs of remote work

Remote and hybrid arrangements can reduce opportunities for spontaneous social interaction. In this study, persistent loneliness was associated with lower subsequent work engagement, and work uncertainty strengthened the relationship between average social distancing and loneliness.

For organizations managing distributed teams, these findings point to a question worth monitoring: how can organizations preserve flexibility while supporting social connection and reducing avoidable uncertainty? Repeated employee listening can help distinguish short-term fluctuations from more persistent experiences.

This study examined social distancing during COVID-19, not a direct comparison of remote, hybrid, and on-site employees. The implications for remote work are applications to consider, not tested effects of remote-work policies.

Original conceptual framework

Sampling and data collection

A probability-based Nebraska panel formed the sampling frame. Baseline and follow-up surveys bracketed 14 daily Qualtrics surveys. The Bureau of Sociological Research supported recruitment, IRB logistics, survey deployment, and participant compensation.

Analysis workflow

I merged and checked 17 datasets, assessed nonresponse bias, variance decomposition, and measurement validity, and estimated multilevel mediation and moderation models with covariates and a lagged loneliness control.

Distinguishing levels

Findings at a glance

Work uncertainty did not significantly moderate the daily distancing–loneliness association, but did moderate the between-person association. The indirect association with engagement was observed at higher—not lower—work uncertainty.

Interpretation limits

The observational design does not establish causality. Any proposed organizational intervention would require additional evaluation. Results should not be presented as evidence of implemented business changes.
